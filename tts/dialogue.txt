SOCRATES: Husserl, you have come to tell me what consciousness is. I have spent my life asking men what they mean by the words they use. So begin simply. When I see this cup, what is actually given?

HUSSERL: Only a profile is strictly seen. The far side is absent, yet intended as belonging to the same cup. Consciousness is always consciousness of something, and every appearance carries a horizon of further possible appearances.

SOCRATES: Then seeing already contains what is absent.

HUSSERL: Absence, yes. Not nothingness.

SOCRATES: You distinguish them quickly. Suppose the whole world were doubtful. Would the appearing also be doubtful?

HUSSERL: That is why I perform the epoché. I do not deny the world; I suspend its unquestioned existence. What remains is the field of appearing itself: perceiving, remembering, judging, imagining, and the objects precisely as they are given.

SOCRATES: So you can bracket the world, but not the fact that something appears. And to whom does it appear?

HUSSERL: To transcendental subjectivity. Not Edmund Husserl as a biological man, but the ego-pole of the stream of experience.

SOCRATES: You have escaped the body remarkably quickly for a man suspicious of my soul. Can this pole be absent while its absence is being considered?

HUSSERL: No. Reflection presupposes the life upon which it reflects. But do not smuggle immortality into that obviousness.

SOCRATES: Good. Let us turn to time. A lyre sounds one note, then another. The first is gone when the second arrives, yet I hear a melody. How?

HUSSERL: Because consciousness is not a row of knife-edge instants. The living present contains primal impression, retention of what has just passed, and protention toward what is coming. Without that structure there would be sounds, perhaps, but no melody.

SOCRATES: And when the melody ends? Something remains after its last note to retain that note as last.

HUSSERL: Yes. The final tone passes into retention, a protention toward continuation remains unfulfilled, and the temporal object is constituted as complete.

SOCRATES: Then let us make consciousness itself the melody. What remains after its final phase to constitute that phase as final?

HUSSERL: Nothing needs to remain. You have shifted from the constitution of an object to the existence of the constituting stream. A stream could simply possess a final phase.

SOCRATES: Could it? Or have you drawn a final mark on a diagram and mistaken your mark for something experienced? At my trial I considered death in two ways: perhaps no perception at all, like dreamless sleep; perhaps the soul goes elsewhere. What do you make of the first?

HUSSERL: Dreamless sleep is a poor model of annihilation. You call the night dreamless only because you awaken. Sleep is a gap inside a continuing life. Absolute cessation would have no awakening from which the gap could be grasped.

SOCRATES: Then the only first-person image we possess for total absence is borrowed from a case where presence returns. And when men imagine annihilation as darkness, silence, emptiness, or endless sleep?

HUSSERL: They turn non-experience into an experience. Darkness and silence are still modes of givenness. Emptiness is still something appearing. Endless sleep still smuggles in a sleeper.

SOCRATES: Then purge the picture. No darkness. No silence. No waiting. No empty duration. No sleeper. No observer whispering, I am gone. What remains?

HUSSERL: No further experience. And no, that no-further-experience is never given, retained, remembered, fulfilled, or encountered by a later phase.

SOCRATES: Then absolute cessation never occupies any position within the stream. It never becomes its past. It never appears to the subject who supposedly ceases. You call that cessation; I call it something that has never been shown as a state of mine.

HUSSERL: There is your illicit inference. Why must the end of a subject be another state for that subject? A lamp does not need to illuminate its own being extinguished. What can be the case need not be what can be given.

SOCRATES: Again you make me a lamp. A lamp is an object that appears. I am asking about that to which the lamp appears. I grant that I experience now does not contain I experience eternally. But neither does it contain I am absolutely absent later.

HUSSERL: Not as present evidence, no. Death as absolute cessation is not a phenomenon like waking, sleeping, remembering, seeing, or recovering from unconsciousness. It is not one more item inside the stream.

SOCRATES: Then it is a boundary-concept spoken from outside the stream. Perhaps we have discovered something smaller than immortality but sharper than ignorance: every ending we actually know is known because some later phase constitutes it as having ended.

HUSSERL: Explain that without rhetoric.

SOCRATES: The melody ends because consciousness retains the final tone and encounters no next tone. A sentence ends because I remain to understand that the final word was final. A pain ends because a later experience retains the pain as no longer occurring. A dream ends because waking consciousness says: that was the dream.

HUSSERL: Continue.

SOCRATES: But if consciousness itself ends absolutely, your hypothesis removes the very later phase by which its ending could ever be constituted as an ending.

HUSSERL: Phenomenologically, yes.

SOCRATES: Then here is the asymmetry. Objects can end within consciousness. But the absolute ending of consciousness cannot appear within the consciousness that ends.

HUSSERL: Yes. But listen carefully, Socrates: that still does not establish another experience.

SOCRATES: No. And now you have wounded my Phaedo.

HUSSERL: As I intended. You argue there from the soul's relation to life toward its refusal of death. But phenomenology cannot authorize that transition merely from the structure of appearing. Essence is not biography. The fact that consciousness is consciousness while it occurs does not promise an endless future.

SOCRATES: Then you have cut away my immortality.

HUSSERL: I have cut away what your premises have not earned.

SOCRATES: Good. A philosopher should be grateful to the knife. Now I cut you. If cessation can never be constituted within subjectivity, what evidence makes absolute cessation the fate of subjectivity itself, rather than merely the disappearance of this organism from the world experienced by others?

HUSSERL: As a phenomenologist? None. The organism can die. Others can observe the body. Its speech can stop. Its movements can stop. Its brain can cease functioning. But none of this converts my absolute nonappearance into an experience belonging to me.

SOCRATES: Then the sentence—later there is no experience—changes its meaning according to the standpoint from which it is spoken.

HUSSERL: Exactly. As a third-person hypothesis, it can be coherent. As a first-person phenomenon, it is impossible.

SOCRATES: There is the wound.

HUSSERL: Not immortality. A limit.

SOCRATES: A limit that can never appear as crossed.

HUSSERL: Yes.

SOCRATES: A supposedly final present that can never be retained as final.

HUSSERL: Yes.

SOCRATES: An ending unlike every ending consciousness has ever known, because it cannot become an ended thing for the one whose ending it supposedly is.

HUSSERL: Yes.

SOCRATES: Then perhaps the first question of death is not: What comes after experience?

HUSSERL: What is it?

SOCRATES: Whether the words after experience have any first-person meaning at all.

HUSSERL: That is the stronger question.

SOCRATES: And rigor requires two refusals. We must not turn absolute absence into a secret experience—

HUSSERL: —and we must not turn the impossibility of experiencing absence into proof that consciousness survives.

SOCRATES: So you deny the annihilationist his imagined nothingness. And deny me an easy immortality.

HUSSERL: I deny both of you more than your evidence warrants.

SOCRATES: Then phenomenology is more Socratic than you admit.

HUSSERL: And your Socrates is rather more phenomenological than Plato might admit.

SOCRATES: Careful. Plato is standing nearby. One final question. If the end of consciousness can never be given, what has someone actually established when he says: For me, there will be nothing?

HUSSERL: Only that, from the standpoint of the world, he posits no later acts attributable to that person. He has not described a future nothingness belonging to the subject.

SOCRATES: Then the honest sentence is not: I will experience nothing.

HUSSERL: No.

SOCRATES: And it is not: I therefore must continue forever.

HUSSERL: No.

SOCRATES: Then perhaps it is this: My absolute absence is not a possible appearance for me, and therefore cannot be known in the same manner as any event occurring within my life.

HUSSERL: Yes.

SOCRATES: Then we have discovered something. The death of the organism may be an event in the world. But the absolute cessation of subjectivity—if it occurs—is not an event in subjective time. It is a limit that can be asserted from outside but never constituted from within.

HUSSERL: And from that limit neither immortality nor annihilation follows by itself.

SOCRATES: So we have not solved death.

HUSSERL: No.

SOCRATES: We have discovered that philosophers often ask the wrong question about it.

HUSSERL: Which question?

SOCRATES: They ask: What will nothingness be like for me?

HUSSERL: When, if it is truly nothingness, there can be no for me.

SOCRATES: Exactly. And whenever they picture their own absence, they restore the witness they intended to abolish.

HUSSERL: While whenever they infer eternal survival from that failure, they mistake a limitation of givenness for a guarantee of being.

SOCRATES: So your knife cuts both ways.

HUSSERL: As it should.

SOCRATES: Then we end not merely in ignorance. We end in a more exact ignorance than the one with which we began.

HUSSERL: That I will accept.

SOCRATES: Good. The disappearance of seeing cannot itself be seen. And every attempt to see it has already failed—by succeeding.
